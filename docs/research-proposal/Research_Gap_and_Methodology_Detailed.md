## 1. Executive Summary

Every supervised programming lab today answers one question reliably — **is the right student physically present?** — using ID cards, biometrics, face match, or RFID. None of them answer a second, quietly more important question: **is that student actually the one producing the code appearing on their screen, right now, in this session?**

This gap is not theoretical. A friend, a senior, or a hired proxy can sit down at a classmate's terminal for the length of a lab period and type a solution live, in full view of an instructor, while the system of record shows the enrolled student as "present" and "authenticated." No plagiarism detector catches this, because the final code is original, unassisted-by-AI, and never copied from anywhere — it was typed by a different human being, live, under the nose of supervision built to prevent exactly this and yet blind to it.

Two research literatures currently sit on either side of this problem without covering it:

- **Process-driven authenticity detection** (keystroke dynamics, edit-sequence analysis) is built for **take-home, asynchronous** cheating — a student alone, at home, with unlimited time, trying to disguise an LLM-generated answer.
- **Physical proxy attendance detection** (RFID, face match, weight sensors) is built to catch impersonation **at a single checkpoint** — someone else swiping a card or matching a face at the door.

Neither is built to observe *what happens at the keyboard for the rest of the session*, which is exactly where a live proxy-typing episode occurs. This proposal's contribution is to instrument that missing middle: an in-lab, process-aware authenticity classifier, validated with a controlled ground-truth study rather than a small proof-of-concept, and positioned explicitly against the closest existing system (Nosi IDE, 2026) as an extension into an adversary setting nobody has tested.

## 2. The Problem, in Detail

Supervised programming labs are a core part of how programming is taught and assessed — but their integrity model has a blind spot that has become more urgent, not less, in the era of take-home LLM misuse crackdowns:

| | What is actually checked | Mechanism |
|---|---|---|
| **Identity at entry** | Is this the enrolled student? | ID card, biometric scan, face match, RFID tap |
| **Presence during the session** | Is the student's body in the room? | Instructor visual sweep, seating charts, periodic roll call |
| **Final code correctness** | Does the submitted code work / match expected output? | Autograders, compiler output, test suites |
| **Final code originality** | Was the code copied from someone else or generated by an LLM? | Plagiarism detectors (MOSS, JPlag), AI-text detectors |
| **Authorship of the live typing process** | ❌ **Not checked by anything above** | — |

The last row is the crux of the problem. Every mechanism institutions currently deploy operates either *before* the session (identity checks) or *on the final artifact* (correctness and originality checks). None of them operate *during* the session on the *process* of producing the code. A proxy typist defeats every layer above simultaneously: they are not on the roll call (the enrolled student is), they produce original code (no plagiarism match), and the code is not LLM output (no AI-detector flag) — it is simply typed by the wrong person, live, under supervision that was never designed to notice.

This is a different adversary model from "cheating" in the sense most institutional tooling assumes, and it requires a different signal to catch: not *what* was produced, but *who* was producing it and *how*, moment to moment.

## 3. Past Work — What Already Exists

The literature relevant to this problem splits cleanly into two active threads plus a shared foundational layer beneath both. This section works through each in turn, what it established, and — critically — what it leaves untouched.

### 3.1 Foundational work — IDE-based learning analytics & process mining

Before either "authenticity detection" thread existed as a labeled research area, twenty years of computing-education research established a simpler and more basic fact: **the process of writing code, not just the final code, carries meaningful behavioral signal.**

| Study | Venue / Year | Focus | Signal used |
|---|---|---|---|
| Jadud | CS Ed, 2005 | Novice compilation behavior | Compile-event streams |
| Blikstein | LAK, 2011 | Behavior in open-ended tasks | Fine-grained trace mining |
| Ihantola et al. | ITiCSE WG, 2015 | Field-wide literature review | Survey of data granularity, 2005–2015 |
| Hundhausen, Olivares & Carter | ACM TOCE, 2017 | IDE-based learning-analytics process model | Collect → analyze → design intervention → deliver intervention |
| Pereira et al. (CodeBench) | 2016–2021 | Early performance prediction | Keystrokes, submissions; 2,058 students, 16 CS1 courses |

**What this established:** Jadud's 2005 work on compile-event streams was arguably the first demonstration that a novice programmer's *trajectory* through errors, not just their eventual success, is diagnostic. Blikstein (2011) extended this into fine-grained trace mining of open-ended tasks, showing behavioral clusters correspond to different learning strategies. By 2015, Ihantola et al.'s working-group survey found the field mature enough to review but noted a **recurring, still-cited limitation**: most studies were single-institution, single-course, and shallow in the metrics they extracted from raw traces — a methodological gap that, notably, still shows up in the newest 2026 work discussed below. Hundhausen, Olivares & Carter's 2017 process model formalized the pipeline (collect → analyze → design intervention → deliver intervention) that essentially every later system, including the one proposed here, still follows. CodeBench (Pereira et al., 2016–2021) is the largest-scale demonstration to date that this kind of fine-grained data — keystrokes and submissions across 2,058 students in 16 CS1 courses — can be collected and used for early performance prediction at real scale.

**Why it matters here:** This body of work is the load-bearing assumption underneath the entire proposal. If fine-grained IDE traces did not carry meaningful signal about *how* a person is working, there would be no basis for believing keystroke- or edit-level data could ever distinguish an authentic session from a proxy-typed one. This foundation makes that signal plausible; it does not yet say anything about authenticity or authorship specifically — that reframing is only five years old.

### 3.2 Recent work — Process-driven authenticity & integrity (2024–2026)

Only in the last two years has this foundational signal been explicitly repurposed for **integrity** rather than *performance prediction* — driven almost entirely by the arrival of LLMs as a take-home cheating vector.

| Study | Venue / Year | Focus | Signal used |
|---|---|---|---|
| Crossley, Tian, Choi, Holmes & Morris | EDM, 2024 | Plagiarism detection | Keystroke logs (take-home) |
| arXiv | 2025 | LLM-assisted academic dishonesty | Keystroke dynamics (TypeNet, LightGBM, CatBoost); F1 > 97%; adversarial "forged keystroke" robustness test |
| **Lueder et al. — Nosi IDE** | **ACM Learning@Scale, 2026** | **Coding-assessment integrity in the age of LLMs** | **Keystroke inter-arrival time (IAT), log-transformed, robust L-scale statistics** |
| — (transformer encoder framework) | Discover Computing, 2026 | Sequence-level behavior modeling | Multi-dimensional transformer encoder over short/medium/long-term event dependencies |

**Crossley et al. (2024)** established, at EDM, that keystroke logs collected during take-home assignments can be used directly as a plagiarism-detection signal — a first explicit bridge from "process data predicts performance" to "process data reveals dishonesty." The 2025 arXiv paper pushed this further and harder: using established keystroke-biometrics architectures (TypeNet) alongside gradient-boosted classifiers (LightGBM, CatBoost), it reports F1 scores above 97% for detecting LLM-assisted dishonesty, and — importantly — includes an adversarial robustness test against "forged keystroke" attacks (an adversary deliberately trying to make typing *look* human-authentic while transcribing an LLM answer). This adversarial angle matters because it is the first sign in the literature that the arms race between detection and evasion is already being anticipated, not just the detection problem itself.

**Nosi IDE (Lueder et al., 2026) is the closest prior system to this proposal and must be read first before any further work begins.** It is a forked VS Code distribution that ships assignments as encrypted, per-student files, logging every edit and paste event. Its central empirical contribution is that **keystroke inter-arrival-time (IAT) statistics — specifically log-transformed, robust L-scale summaries of typing rhythm — reliably separate "scribing" (a student manually retyping an LLM's output) from authentic typing.** This result comes from a small proof-of-concept with **N = 5** participants, which is explicitly framed by the authors as a first demonstration rather than a validated deployment.

The 2026 Discover Computing transformer-encoder paper generalizes the modeling approach rather than the application: it models fine-grained student programming behavior as a sequence with short-, medium-, and long-term dependencies, using a multi-dimensional transformer encoder. This is architecturally more sophisticated than the L-scale statistics in Nosi IDE, but it is applied to the same broad class of problem (modeling behavior over a session) rather than specifically to live authorship verification.

**The critical implication for this proposal:** the framing "watch the process, not just the final artifact, to catch inauthentic work" is *not* a novel pitch anymore. It was published five months before this proposal was written, at a top-tier venue (ACM Learning@Scale), with a working system. Any thesis operating in this space must position itself explicitly *against* Nosi IDE — as an extension, a stress test, or a boundary case — rather than re-presenting the same framing as if it were new. This proposal does exactly that (see Sections 4–6).

### 3.3 Physical attendance & identity verification

Running in parallel, and almost entirely disconnected from the process-driven literature above, is a body of applied and commercial work on **physical presence and identity verification** — none of which touches coding behavior at all.

| Study / System | Venue / Year | Focus | What it verifies |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ege & Özdemir | arXiv, 2026 | Biometric-free IoT attendance | RFID card + weight-sensor match against a statistical reference range |
| Vizenta-style CCTV systems | Commercial, 2025–26 | Facial-recognition roll call | Face match at entry/exit |
| NFC / X-Bee systems | Yeboah-Boateng et al. | Remote-database check-in | Card-tag identity at a reader |
| "Zero-Proxy" facial auth | Practitioner project, 2026 | Liveness-gated face check-in | Presence + liveness, once, at entry |

Ege & Özdemir's 2026 system is a representative and recent example: it pairs an RFID card read with a weight-sensor measurement, checking that the person tapping in has a body weight statistically consistent with the enrolled student's stored reference range — a clever, biometric-free way to catch a proxy who has borrowed someone's ID card. Commercial CCTV roll-call systems and NFC/X-Bee check-in systems solve a related but narrower problem: confirming a face or tag matches a database entry at a reader. A newer practitioner pattern, "Zero-Proxy" facial authentication, adds a liveness check (to defeat photo/video spoofing) but is, like all the systems in this table, a **single-checkpoint** verification — it runs once, at entry (or occasionally at a periodic re-scan), and has nothing to say about the subsequent one to three hours of a lab session.

**The load-bearing observation from this thread:** every system here verifies presence at a checkpoint. None of them — not one — observes what happens at the keyboard afterward. This is precisely where a live proxy-typing episode would occur, and precisely what this literature has no instrumentation for.

### 3.4 What's confirmed vs. unconfirmed vs. genuinely open

Pulling the three threads together, it is worth being explicit about which claims are well-established, which are plausible-but-untested, and which are genuinely open — because a thesis proposal should not overclaim novelty where prior work already covers the ground, nor underclaim it where a real gap exists.

| Claim | Status | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| CodeBench collected fine-grained data from 2,058 students, 16 CS1 courses | ✅ **Confirmed** | Directly reported in Pereira et al. |
| Sequential/clustering analysis of programming behavior trajectories predicts outcomes | ✅ **Confirmed** | Bey et al.; the 2026 transformer-encoder paper |
| AST-evolution / structural-jump detection for pasted code, as a live anomaly signal within one session | ⚠️ **Existing tools don't cover this specific application** | CP-Miner, CReN, jsinspect detect clones *across a codebase*, not a sudden structural jump *within one live session* — this specific application appears open |
| Proxy-attendance research uses IDE process data | ❌ **Not found** | All proxy-attendance work surveyed is biometric/RFID/facial — confirms the gap directly |

The AST-jump row deserves a brief technical note: tools like CP-Miner, CReN, and jsinspect are built for **cross-codebase clone detection** — finding that file A and file B share structurally similar code, typically across a large corpus, offline. None of them are built to watch a *single evolving file* within *one session* and flag the moment a large, structurally coherent block of code appears in a way inconsistent with the edit history leading up to it (e.g., a near-complete, syntactically clean function appearing between two keystroke timestamps that are only a few hundred milliseconds apart, with no preceding scaffolding). That specific real-time, single-session application of AST-diffing appears genuinely unaddressed in the surveyed literature — it is closer to an available building block (AST diffing is mature technology) applied to a new problem, than to a new algorithm.

## 4. The Research Gap

```
 Process-driven authenticity          Physical proxy attendance
 detection (take-home, async)         detection (identity at check-in)
                \                            /
                 \                          /
                  \                        /
                   ▼                      ▼
        ┌─────────────────────────────────────────┐
        │              UNADDRESSED                 │
        │  Live, in-person authorship verification  │
        │       in a supervised lab session         │
        └─────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

Stated plainly: **neither literature covers a student who is physically present and identity-verified, but where someone else is doing the actual coding, live, under supervision, in real time.** Thread 1 assumes an adversary alone, unsupervised, with unlimited time to disguise their work — a fundamentally different pressure environment from a lab. Thread 2 assumes the only moment of vulnerability is a single checkpoint at entry, and has no answer for what happens for the remaining ninety minutes of the session.

This is not a hypothetical edge case invented to justify a thesis. It is the everyday, low-tech, socially-engineered failure mode of any supervised assessment that verifies identity once and then stops watching — a friend, a senior, or a paid proxy sitting down at a terminal that has already passed its identity check, and typing.

## 5. Comparative Positioning

The table below makes explicit how the proposed study differs from both existing threads along every dimension that matters for study design — adversary, setting, signal, and what ground truth has been used so far.

| Dimension | Process-Driven Work (Nosi IDE, keystroke-dishonesty papers) | Attendance Systems (RFID / biometric) | **Proposed Study** |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Adversary model** | Solo student, at home, unlimited time | Someone else checks in for you | Someone else types for you, live, in the room |
| **Setting** | Take-home / asynchronous | Entry checkpoint only | Supervised, in-session lab |
| **Signal** | Keystroke IAT, edit sequences | Face / RFID / weight | Keystroke IAT + edit sequence + AST structural jumps |
| **Time pressure on adversary** | None (can disguise at leisure) | N/A | High — must perform under instructor visibility |
| **Ground truth used so far** | N = 5 proof-of-concept (Nosi) | Sensor accuracy benchmarks | Controlled in-lab groups: genuine / proxy-typed / minimal-interaction |
| **Open question** | Does it generalize beyond take-home? | Does not consider authorship at all | Do take-home signals transfer to live, in-person proxy typing? |

The row worth dwelling on is **"time pressure on adversary."** In the take-home setting, an adversary disguising an LLM answer can retype it slowly, deliberately, across an afternoon, mimicking natural rhythm at leisure — the exact behavior the 2025 arXiv paper's "forged keystroke" robustness test anticipates. A live, in-lab proxy typist has no such luxury: they must produce a plausible solution, at a plausible pace, while a real person's presence is being visually monitored, within a fixed lab period. This asymmetry cuts both ways scientifically — it could make detection easier (less room for the adversary to consciously disguise their rhythm) or harder (the adversary is under enough real cognitive load that their typing may look more "naturally effortful," resembling genuine struggle rather than confident scribing). Which of these dominates is precisely the empirical question this proposal is designed to answer, and it is not derivable from the take-home literature — it requires new data collected under the live, supervised condition.

## 6. Research Question

> **Can IDE-level process signals — keystroke timing, edit/execution sequences, and structural code jumps — distinguish a student's own authentic in-lab work from proxy-typed or minimally-engaged work, and do they generalize better or worse than the equivalent signals already shown to detect take-home LLM-scribing?**

This question is deliberately built with two layers, not one:

1. **A detection layer** — can the proposed feature set (keystroke IAT, edit-sequence shape, AST structural jumps) separate genuine, proxy-typed, and minimal-interaction sessions at all, in a live in-lab setting?
2. **A transfer layer** — do the *specific* signals already validated by Nosi IDE and the 2024–25 keystroke-dishonesty papers (built and tuned for take-home scribing) carry over to this new adversary setting, or does the live, supervised, time-pressured context change their statistical behavior enough that they need to be re-derived?

This second layer is what makes the question a genuine extension of prior work rather than a restatement of it. It positions the thesis directly against Lueder et al. (2026, Nosi IDE) and the 2024–25 keystroke-dishonesty line of work, and — importantly — **either answer is a publishable result.** If the signals transfer cleanly, that is a strong generalization result strengthening the case for a unified process-authenticity framework across settings. If they do not transfer, that is an equally important boundary-condition finding: it would mean the community's leading detection signal is setting-specific in a way not previously tested, and that live supervised environments need their own feature engineering rather than borrowing take-home features wholesale.

## 7. Proposed Work — How the Gap Will Be Addressed

### 7.1 System: A process-instrumented lab IDE

The proposed system is a four-stage pipeline, designed to sit inside lab workstations that are already under instructor supervision — a deliberate contrast to Nosi IDE's take-home, encrypted-file distribution model.

| Stage | What it does |
|---|---|
| **1. Event Collector** | Captures keystrokes, pastes, runs, edits, and timestamps during the lab session |
| **2. Feature Extraction** | Computes keystroke inter-arrival time (IAT), edit-sequence shape, and AST-diff structural jumps |
| **3. Authenticity Classifier** | Classifies each session as genuine, proxy-typed, or minimal-interaction |
| **4. Instructor Dashboard** | Surfaces a session-level flag with a supporting evidence trace |

Each stage maps to a specific engineering and research decision:

- **Event Collector** is intentionally the least novel part of the system — it mirrors what Nosi IDE and CodeBench already do (log keystrokes, pastes, and run events with timestamps), because there is no reason to re-invent event logging. The novelty in this proposal is entirely downstream of this stage.
- **Feature Extraction** carries over the two feature families already validated for take-home detection (keystroke IAT from Nosi IDE; edit-sequence shape as a generalization of the edit-sequence work in the 2024–25 papers), and adds **AST-diff structural jumps** — a feature family motivated directly by the "existing tools don't cover this" gap identified in Section 3.4. Concretely, this means diffing the abstract syntax tree of the file before and after each edit event, and flagging edits that introduce a large, structurally coherent, syntactically complete block of code in a single step inconsistent with the preceding edit history (as opposed to gradual, incremental construction).
- **Authenticity Classifier** is a three-way classifier (not binary), because "minimal-interaction" (pasting a near-complete solution with little iteration) is a meaningfully different failure mode from "proxy-typed" (a second person typing character-by-character) — they may produce very different IAT and AST-jump signatures, and collapsing them into a single "not authentic" label would throw away diagnostic information that matters both for the science and for how an instructor should respond.
- **Instructor Dashboard** is the human-facing endpoint: a session-level flag is only useful pedagogically if it comes with an evidence trace an instructor can inspect and act on directly (e.g., "large AST jump at timestamp X, followed by an atypical IAT distribution for the remainder of the session") rather than an opaque score.

**Key design choice:** unlike Nosi IDE (encrypted, take-home, N = 5), this system runs on lab workstations already under instructor supervision. This is what makes a **controlled, multi-group ground-truth study** possible in the first place, instead of a small, uncontrolled proof-of-concept — the lab setting provides a natural venue for recruiting participants into known experimental conditions, something take-home deployments structurally cannot offer without extraordinary effort.

### 7.2 Controlled ground-truth study design

The central methodological contribution of this proposal is replacing Nosi IDE's small (N = 5) proof-of-concept with a **controlled, labeled, multi-group study**, run directly in the lab environment the system targets.

| Group | Condition |
|---|---|
| **A — Genuine** | Student solves the lab exercise themselves, under normal conditions |
| **B — Proxy-Typed** | A second person types the solution live at the student's terminal |
| **C — Minimal Interaction** | Student pastes/inserts a near-complete solution with little iteration |

This design gives every session a **known ground-truth label at collection time** — something neither Nosi IDE's small proof-of-concept nor any of the attendance-system literature provides. Group A establishes the authentic baseline distribution of the process signals. Group B is the adversary condition central to the research question — a second person genuinely typing the solution live, reproducing the real-world scenario this proposal is built around, rather than simulating it after the fact. Group C is included specifically because it is easy to conflate with Group B if the feature set is not discriminating enough: a student who pastes a near-complete solution is *not* being proxy-typed, but might trigger similar AST-jump signals, so Group C exists to test whether the classifier can tell these two failure modes apart rather than lumping all "non-genuine" sessions together.

### 7.3 Validation layers

A three-way ground-truth label alone is not sufficient evidence that the system works in practice — labels assigned by experimental design need to be checked against independent signals, and the system's real-world usefulness needs to be tested beyond the immediate session. Three validation layers are proposed:

1. **Controlled sessions** (Section 7.2) — the known ground-truth label per session, assigned by experimental design.
2. **Independent instructor labeling** on a subset — instructors, blind to the experimental group assignment, label sessions as genuine / suspicious / unclear, providing an external check on whether the statistical classification aligns with what a human observer in the room would actually notice.
3. **Held-out problem retest** — after the lab session, can the student solve a similar (but not identical) problem independently? This is a proxy for whether "genuine" sessions actually reflect learning and whether flagged sessions correspond to a gap in the student's own understanding, connecting the authenticity signal back to an educationally meaningful outcome rather than treating detection as an end in itself.

### 7.4 Ethics Note

Keystroke logs can act as a biometric identifier — inter-key timing patterns are individually distinctive enough to be used for re-identification in their own right, independent of the content typed. This has concrete consequences for how this study must be run:

- A **de-identification plan** is required before any data collection, separating the biometric-adjacent keystroke stream from directly identifying metadata wherever possible.
- **IRB / ethics board approval is required before any deployment** — this is not optional given the biometric-adjacent nature of the data and the fact that Group B sessions involve a second, non-enrolled participant whose typing is also being recorded.
- Any published dataset arising from this work needs the **same safeguards already discussed in prior surveys of keystroke data in programming courses** — this proposal does not treat data-sharing ethics as a novel problem to solve from scratch, but commits to following established norms from the field it is extending.

### 7.5 Scope discipline

It is worth being explicit about a scoping decision, because the temptation in a problem area this rich is to over-promise. The original pitch this proposal grew out of suggested **five parallel research models** — engagement scoring, authenticity detection, learning-outcome prediction, AST fingerprinting, and a full intervention loop — deployed across 100–300 students for 8–12 weeks. That is, realistically, multiple papers' worth of work, and treating it as a single M.Tech thesis would risk a shallow result across five fronts rather than a rigorous one on any single front.

This methodology deliberately narrows the thesis to **one core, defensible contribution: the controlled authenticity-classification study described in Sections 7.1–7.3.** Engagement modeling and learning-outcome prediction are explicitly retained as **clearly labeled future work**, not promised deliverables — a distinction that matters both for realistic thesis planning and for setting correct expectations with an advisor or committee about what this work will and will not claim to have shown.

## 8. Expected Contributions

1. **First controlled comparison** of take-home authenticity signals (keystroke IAT) against a live, in-person proxy-typing adversary — directly answering whether Nosi IDE's core result generalizes beyond the setting it was validated in.
2. **A ground-truth methodology** using in-lab controlled groups (Section 7.2) instead of small asynchronous proof-of-concept studies — a methodological upgrade applicable beyond this specific thesis to future process-authenticity research generally.
3. **An empirical answer to whether existing keystroke/process features transfer across adversary settings** — a positive result strengthens the case for a unified process-authenticity framework; a null result is an equally valuable boundary-condition finding about the setting-specificity of the field's leading detection signal.
4. **A scoped, deployable instructor-facing flagging tool** for supervised programming labs specifically — distinct from Nosi IDE's take-home use case, and designed from the outset to produce an evidence trace an instructor can act on, not just a score.

## 9. Risks and Mitigations

| Risk | Why it matters | Mitigation |
|---|---|---|
| **Group B (proxy-typed) sessions may not generalize to real-world proxy typists** | Volunteer "proxies" in a controlled study know they are being studied and may behave differently from a real, covert proxy | Recruit proxies with varying familiarity with the material; compare high- and low-skill proxy conditions; treat this as a documented limitation rather than an unaddressed threat to validity |
| **Small sample size relative to deep-learning feature extraction** | The transformer-encoder approach (Discover Computing, 2026) suggests more data-hungry models are being used in this space | Prefer interpretable, lower-parameter statistics (IAT L-scale statistics, as in Nosi IDE) as the primary classifier, with more complex models treated as a stretch goal, not the core deliverable |
| **Ethics/IRB approval timeline** | Keystroke data collection involving a second, non-enrolled participant (the proxy) may require broader consent processes | Begin IRB engagement early, in parallel with system development, rather than sequentially after the system is built |
| **AST-diff feature may be noisy for legitimate large edits** (e.g., a student pasting their own boilerplate) | Could inflate false positives if not carefully tuned | Use Group C (minimal-interaction, self-pasted) specifically to characterize and separate this failure mode from genuine proxy typing during feature validation |
| **Nosi IDE or a similar system may publish a live-lab extension before this thesis completes** | The field is moving fast (2026 is the most active year in this table) | Treat this as evidence the problem is real and worth solving, not a reason to abandon it; differentiate on the controlled multi-group ground-truth methodology even if the application target converges |
